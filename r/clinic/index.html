One system, running live

The call gets answered before reception sits down.

This one is running — for a clinic in Delhi NCR, right now, every day. A voice AI picks up the phone itself, talks to the patient, and books the appointment. Five agents, one number I actually measured, and a calculator below that runs your numbers instead of mine.

Here's what actually happens now:The phone rings at 9:40 at night. It gets answered — in Hindi, in English, or the mix most people actually speak. The caller says what they need, hears when there's a slot, and takes one. By the time somebody unlocks the clinic in the morning, that appointment is already in the calendar with the patient's details against it.
Problem

Good footfall, expensive treatments, and a phone nobody could always reach

Nothing was wrong with the front of this clinic. The treatments were priced properly, patients were walking in, the phone was ringing.

The gap was the phone itself. Reception was with a patient at the counter, or two calls came at once, or it was 9pm and the clinic was shut. So a share of the calls simply didn't get answered.

In a clinic that isn't the same thing as a missed call in a shop. Somebody ringing a clinic has usually already decided something — that it hurts enough, that it needs looking at, that they're finally going to do it. A missed call there isn't a missed call. It's a missed treatment, and treatments here aren't small.

The client can name one. A client worth about a lakh went somewhere else because nobody picked up the phone. Not price, not the doctor, not a bad experience. Nobody picked up.

And this is the part that makes it hard to catch: nothing looks broken. No complaint, no error, no empty row in a register. A call that didn't get answered leaves nothing behind — no name, no number, no note about what they wanted. You lose them every week and the register never tells you.

System

Five agents, and the phone never rings out

One job each. A call coming in is the only thing that starts it — nobody presses anything.

  1. Agent 1

    Answers the call and books the slot

    On the first ring, at any hour. In Hindi, in English, or switching between the two the way people actually talk. It asks what the patient needs, tells them what's open, and books the appointment while they're still on the line.

    Ten calls landing at once is ten calls answered. Nobody gets a busy tone, and nobody gets a ring that goes on until they give up.

  2. Agent 2

    Captures what the caller actually wanted

    Not a recording, and not a transcript nobody will read. What they asked about, what they were worried about, whether they've been in before, what was agreed — written as the call happens, ready the moment it ends.

    This is what makes the callback the next morning a real conversation instead of asking the patient to explain themselves a second time.

  3. Agent 3

    Writes to the CRM and catches slot clashes

    The appointment, the patient's details and the notes go where they belong. If two people ask for the same slot, it sees the clash itself and offers the second caller the nearest open one instead of quietly taking both.

    You don't get the awkward phone call the next day, because the double booking didn't happen.

  4. Agent 4

    Sends the WhatsApp to the no-shows

    Booked and didn't turn up gets a WhatsApp. One line, no guilt trip, and an easy way to take another slot.

    This is the job everybody intends to do and nobody does, because by the evening the clinic has been full for six hours and you've forgotten who didn't come.

  5. Agent 5

    Produces the weekly report

    Calls in, appointments booked, no-shows, and what people are asking about most. That last one is the useful one.

    If thirty callers a week ask the same question, that isn't a phone problem — it's something missing from your page, your board, or your Google listing.

Result

The one number I measured

Missed calls went to nearly zero. Same clinic, same team, same treatments — the only thing that changed is that the phone stopped going unanswered.

That's the number, and it's the only one going on this page. Not because it's the most impressive one, but because it's the one I can stand behind.

So let me say what I didn't measure. Consultations went up after this — that's what the client tells me, and I believe them. But I don't have a clean before-and-after count I would defend, so there is no booking figure on this page, no percentage, and no revenue number anywhere on it. If a page like this shows you a 3x, the fair question is where that number came from and who counted it.

What can be said fairly is narrower and more useful. This system changed one thing: whether the phone gets answered. Whether an answered call becomes a treatment depends on your prices, your doctors, and what happens once the patient walks in — and none of that is something software does for you. That's also why the calculator sits higher up this page instead of a result here. The honest version of what this is worth is the one built out of your own figures, not mine.

Missed calls are the part I can prove. Everything downstream of the phone is your clinic, not my software.

Requirements

What this needs on your side

Three things have to be true before any of the above can run. If they aren't, this is a setup project first and an automation second — better to know that now than three weeks in.

  1. Line

    A phone line that can be routed

    The number your patients already ring has to be able to forward somewhere — a cloud number, an IVR, a SIP trunk, whatever your operator supports. Usually that's one call to your telecom provider, not a new number.

    The number itself doesn't change, and shouldn't. Handing patients a new number to memorise undoes the whole point of this.

  2. Calendar

    A calendar or CRM with real availability in it

    Something a machine can read and write: which doctor sits when, how long each kind of appointment takes, which slots are already gone. It doesn't have to be expensive software. It has to be current.

    If the real calendar lives in somebody's head and the software version is two days behind, the AI will book on top of patients who are already coming. That's worse than a missed call, not better.

  3. People

    Somebody to settle the slot rules once

    How long a first consult runs, what can't be booked back to back, which slots stay free for emergencies, who can be double-booked and who never is. An hour or two with whoever actually runs the front desk.

    Skip this and every rule gets learnt the expensive way — from the first patient it goes wrong for.

Fit

When this doesn't work

If the practice is walk-in only, this does nothing for you. There's no slot to book and no calendar to protect, so all it can do is take a message — and you don't need five agents for that. It's a perfectly good way to run a clinic. It just isn't the one this is built for.

If your treatments can't be scheduled until a consult has happened, be careful what you let it book. It can book the consult, and that part is clean. It should not be putting a procedure in the calendar off the back of a phone conversation, because the person who decides that hasn't seen the patient yet. Set it to book the first appointment only, and let the clinical call stay clinical.

And one that isn't about tooling at all. If your receptionist also does the billing, chases the payments and holds the calendar, and isn't willing to hand that calendar over — don't start here. Not because they're wrong to be protective. Because the system needs one true version of what's booked, and you can't have that while the real version stays with somebody who wants to keep it. That's a conversation to have inside the clinic before it's a project to buy.

If one of those describes your clinic, this isn't the thing to buy first. I'd rather say that on a page than after an invoice.

Honest

When it sounds wrong — and sometimes it does

Voice AI on an Indian phone line is not the demo you've seen on the internet. That demo was recorded on a good mic in a quiet room. Your patients ring from an auto, from a market, with a TV going, on a line that's already dropping words, in accents the model has heard far less of than it has heard American English. Some of those calls do not go smoothly.

This is what going wrong actually looks like. It mishears a name and repeats it back wrong. It asks the caller to say the date again. It hears a colony name it doesn't know and hesitates for a second too long. And occasionally somebody speaks in a way it can't follow at all — a strong regional accent over a bad line is the hardest case, and around Delhi NCR it is not rare.

So the thing that matters isn't whether it fails. It's what happens when it does. When it can't understand, it transfers to a human, or takes the number and flags the call for a callback that morning. What it must never do is ask the same question a third time. Loop a patient twice and they hang up — and now you've built a machine that misses calls more politely than a ringing phone did.

The other failure has nothing to do with accuracy. If it sounds like a recording — the flat IVR voice, the pause before every reply, the too-correct Hindi nobody actually speaks — patients hang up even when it understood them perfectly. That's why so much of the work goes into how it talks rather than what it knows. A bot that gets the booking right and sounds like a machine still loses the patient.

None of this is a reason not to do it. It's the reason to test it on your own line, with your own patients, before you point the main number at it — and to keep the human path open behind it permanently, not just for the first month.

The question to ask any voice AI vendor isn't how good the demo sounds. It's what the system does with the call it can't understand.
